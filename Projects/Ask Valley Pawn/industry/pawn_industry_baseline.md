## What pawn shops typically take — categories
Pawn shops generally loan on and buy items that hold resale value and can be tested in the store: gold, silver and platinum jewelry; diamonds and gemstone jewelry; coins and bullion; luxury watches (Rolex, Omega, Tudor, Cartier, Breitling, TAG Heuer, Patek Philippe, Audemars Piguet); firearms (at a licensed FFL store); power tools and hand tools; laptops, tablets, TVs and game consoles; musical instruments and audio gear; sporting goods, golf clubs, bows; cameras and lenses; designer handbags; collectibles such as trading cards and sports memorabilia. Whatever the category, the item has to work, be testable or authenticable at the counter, and have a resale market we can comp. Valley Pawn's own "we don't take" rules (for example cell phones, DVDs, sunglasses, phone cases, NiCd-battery tools, items we can't test) override this list.

## Luxury watches and Rolex — yes, they are some of the best items to take
Luxury watches, Rolex above all, are among the most valuable and liquid items a pawn shop sees. Rolex holds value better than almost any other watch brand. The questions are whether it's genuine, what model and condition it is, and whether it comes with box and papers.

## Rolex — how to check it at the counter
1. Get the model (reference) number and serial number. On most Rolex the reference is engraved between the lugs at 12 o'clock and the serial between the lugs at 6 o'clock (bracelet removed); on watches from the late 2000s on, the serial is engraved on the inner bezel ring (rehaut) at 6 o'clock, with "ROLEX ROLEX" engraved around the rehaut.
2. Second hand: a Rolex automatic sweeps smoothly. A ticking one-second jump means quartz — a red flag on almost every Rolex model.
3. Date models: the cyclops lens magnifies the date about 2.5x and the date sits centered under it. A small, weak magnification is a common fake tell.
4. Caseback: on nearly every Rolex the caseback is solid and plain. A clear see-through caseback is a red flag (only a few recent dress and limited models have one).
5. Weight and finish: genuine Rolex feels solid and heavy for its size; engravings and dial printing are crisp, with no blurry text or misaligned markers. Modern Rolex have a tiny crown etched in the crystal at 6 o'clock.
6. Aftermarket parts (diamond bezels, dials, bracelets added later) lower value even on a real watch. Ask.
7. Box, papers (warranty card) and service receipts add real value and help prove ownership.
8. If anything doesn't add up, don't put a number on it — follow our high-value item and worksheet rules and get approval.

## Luxury watches — comping and value
Comp luxury watches by exact reference number and condition from completed sales (eBay sold listings, Chrono24 asking prices as a ceiling). Full set (box and papers) brings noticeably more than watch-only. Condition, polishing, service history and whether it runs and keeps time all move the number. Fashion brands (Michael Kors, Fossil, Invicta and similar) have little resale value compared with luxury Swiss brands.

## Gold, silver and platinum stamps — what the marks mean
Gold karat stamps: 10K = 417, 14K = 585, 18K = 750, 22K = 916, 24K = 999. Plated or filled gold is NOT solid gold and is marked GP, GE, HGE, RGP (plated) or GF, 1/20 12K GF (gold-filled). Sterling silver is marked 925 or STERLING; "coin silver" is about 90%. Platinum is marked PT, PLAT or 950/900 PLAT. Stamps can be faked, so a stamp is a starting point, never proof — always test.

## Trading cards (Pokémon, sports, Magic) — how to evaluate
1. Identify it: the set, the card number (bottom corner), the name, language, and edition. For Pokémon, a "1st Edition" stamp on early sets and holographic (holo) or special rarity cards are what carry value; most common cards are worth very little.
2. Condition drives value: check corners, edges, surface scratches and centering under good light. Any crease or bend drops value sharply.
3. Graded cards (PSA, BGS/Beckett, CGC): look up the certification number on the grader's website and make sure the card and grade in the case match. Check the case isn't cracked or tampered with.
4. Comp it: eBay sold listings for the exact card and grade, plus TCGplayer market price or PriceCharting. Use sold prices, not asking prices.
5. Fake tells: wrong font or colors, glossy or flimsy card stock, holo pattern that looks flat or wrong, blurry print, and a card that lets too much light through when held to a flashlight.
6. Bulk boxes of commons are near-worthless; don't let a big stack drive the number. Sealed booster boxes and packs have value but are easy to reseal — check the wrap.

## Sports memorabilia and autographs
Autographs are only worth real money with authentication from a recognized service (PSA/DNA, JSA, Beckett). Verify the certificate or sticker number on that service's website. An unauthenticated signature should be valued as if it isn't there. Game-used claims need documentation.

## Electronics — general checks (laptops, tablets, consoles, TVs)
Power it on and test it fully: screen, ports, charging, Wi-Fi, buttons, and for consoles, play a game and test the controllers. Make sure it's signed out of the owner's account and has no activation, iCloud, Google or BIOS lock — a locked device can't be resold. Record the serial number. Older generations drop in value quickly once a new model is out. Valley Pawn's own rules on specific devices (cell phones, unsupported Apple devices, original Xbox 360, routers) come first.

## Power tools
Test every tool under power. Check the brand (major pro brands like DeWalt, Milwaukee, Makita hold value best), the battery health and the charger. Kits with batteries and charger bring more than bare tools. Valley Pawn's tool brand list and battery rules come first.

## Musical instruments
Check brand, model and serial number (Gibson, Fender, Martin, Taylor and similar hold value best), playability, cracks in the body or neck, neck straightness, electronics on electric instruments, and whether it has its original case. Comp from eBay sold listings and Reverb sold prices.

## Firearms — general
Firearm transactions are federally regulated and require an FFL, a Form 4473 on every transfer to a non-licensee, and a background check. Always clear and check a firearm before handling it or handing it to anyone. Valley Pawn's firearm procedures in the P&P manual and Academy Level 6 are the rule for every step.

## Every deal — the basics
Get a valid photo ID and record it, photograph the item, write up a detailed description (brand, model, serial number, condition, markings), and follow the stolen-property and reporting rules. If something feels off — the customer doesn't know basic facts about the item, it's new in box in bulk, or the serial is removed or altered — stop and get a manager. Never let a customer rush you past testing.
