# Voice clone recording script — Joshua

Set Voice Memos to Lossless. Phone 6–8 inches away, same spot each take. Quiet, soft room. Read like you're talking to a new hire at the counter, not reading. Stumble? Pause two seconds and repeat the sentence — don't stop the recording. Each take is about 5 minutes. Name each memo "Take 1", "Take 2", etc.

## Take 1 — Welcome & the basics (friendly, like greeting a new hire)  (~5 min)

Welcome to Valley Pawn. We started in 2000, and in 2015 the current owners bought the Staunton store with a plan to grow across the Valley. Today there are five stores: Culpeper, Waynesboro, Harrisonburg, Lexington and Roanoke. Every one of them is a licensed pawnbroker and a federal firearms licensee, and every one of them is Valley Pawn. One name, no matter which store you're in.

Here's the standard. Nobody gets six feet in the door without being greeted. You stand up, you say welcome to Valley Pawn, and you mean it. If you're already helping someone, you still look up and let the new person know you'll be right with them. Every customer, every visit, hears the three things that set us apart: a thirty day warranty on everything we sell, the mobile app to shop and pay, and free layaway. And remember, the thing that loses customers isn't rudeness. It's indifference. Your paycheck and mine come from the people walking through that door.

The chain of command is short. You report to your Store Manager. Store Managers report to Preston Peters, the Market Manager. Preston reports to Joshua Davis, the owner and CEO. Take questions and problems to your Store Manager first. If it isn't resolved in a reasonable time, go up one level. If the problem is about your Store Manager, it goes to Preston. And if something is very serious or private, you can go straight to Joshua.

Store hours are ten to six, and you're in the building by nine thirty to get ready. Culpeper is open Monday through Saturday. The other four stores are closed on Wednesday. Everybody's closed Sunday. And on the first and third Monday of the month there's a nine A M team meeting by Zoom from each store. That's paid time, and if you're scheduled that morning, you're there.

This is how the Academy works. Levels unlock one at a time, in order, and Level one has to be done by the end of your third day. Every lesson ends in a short test. Eighty percent or better unlocks the next one. You get two tries. If you miss twice, your manager gets a nudge to sit with you before you go again, and that's a good thing, not a punishment. Some skills can't be tested on a screen, like counting a drawer. Those lessons end with a Floor Check: your manager watches you do it for real and marks it off. Screen test plus Floor Check means you've mastered it.

You've got two documents. The Employee Handbook is the employment side: pay, time off, conduct, benefits. The Policies and Procedures Manual, which everybody calls the P and P, is how the stores actually run: cash, loans, firearms, the phone, the customer. If it's an employment question, the Handbook controls. If it's a store procedure, the P and P controls. Both are in Gusto under Documents, and you can open them any time.

When you're not sure what the rule is, your first stop is the ask handbook channel in Slack. Type the question the way you'd ask a coworker. It answers only from the Handbook and the P and P, and it tells you which section the answer came from, so you can go read it. If the rule isn't written down, it says not covered, ask your Store Manager, and that's the honest answer. It doesn't guess. It's not for things like the Wi Fi password or your schedule. And if your pay is wrong, that goes straight to Preston or Joshua, not the bot.

In Gusto you'll sign one acknowledgment that covers both documents. It says you received them, you read them, you understand them, and you agree to follow them. If anything is unclear, ask before you sign. It also says employment is at will, for you and for the company. It says the company can change its policies, and when a new version comes out you'll sign again. And it includes your duty to speak up: if you believe something breaks the law or a policy, especially around firearms, cash, or customer records, you report it, and nobody can retaliate against you for doing that.

## Take 2 — The phone & the counter (clear, confident)  (~5 min)

The phone is where a store shines or bombs, and every store line is recorded. Assume every word is on tape, because it is. The call is the customer's first impression. Answer within two rings, give your name, ask for theirs. Here's what one week of our own calls showed: almost every caller who got a number said they were coming in. Almost none of the callers told to text photos ever did, and we hadn't taken their name, so they were gone. Three rules carry this whole lesson. Verify before you disclose. Quote or capture. And never confirm a firearm.

Rule one. Customer information leaves this building only when the law or the manual says it can. Never because a caller sounds like they know the account. Before you give a balance, a due date, what's on the ticket, or whether an account even exists, you need the name plus one thing a stranger wouldn't know: the ticket number, the date of birth, or the last four of the phone number on file. A last name is not verification. And account details go to the account holder, and only the account holder. An ex, a cousin or a friend calling for them is not the customer, even if they know the ticket number. If it doesn't check out, the line is: I can't go over account details on the phone, but come in with your I D and we'll take care of it. Nobody has ever been hurt by that sentence.

Rule two is rule one's most dangerous case. You never confirm over the phone that a specific firearm is in the store, is on a loan, or belongs to a named person. Not to anyone, verified or not. That's a security problem on top of a privacy problem: you've just told a stranger there's a gun in the building and roughly where. Firearm redemption and status conversations happen in person, with I D. The one exception is background check status, to the buyer only, after you've verified them. On a real call last month an employee told a caller who'd given nothing but a name, yes sir, the Glock thirty gen five, it's still here. Don't be that call.

Rule three is the money one. Nobody who asks what we'd pay for something hangs up with nothing. Every sell side call ends with one of three things: a conditional range, a booked time, or a name and a callback number. Ranges are encouraged. Roughly this much to that much depending on condition, I'd need to see it. Ask about the item. If someone has nine Switch games, ask the titles. It all depends is not an answer, it's a goodbye. And for gold, silver, platinum and diamonds, no number unless you have the spot sheet open and you know what the item is. Otherwise say: I don't want to guess and cost you money. Bring it in and we'll weigh and test it while you wait.

Rule four: what you never say. You never name the owner, Preston, or a coworker as the reason for anything. The answer is, our policy is, full stop. You never tell a customer who got fired or transferred, who's working today, that you're by yourself, or how much cash you've got. And you never explain how we handle cash. No bank runs, no let me see if we've got enough. If someone asks about a big loan, the answer is: yes, we can do that loan. Bring it in. On real calls last month a customer was told the manager had been fired, another was told the owner was the reason his loan was handled a certain way, and one caller in the parking lot was told the employee was alone in the store. And you never speculate about a customer on any line, not even to a coworker next to you. The line's open. Last one: you never tell a customer they owe a balance or that their credit is affected. A pawn loan is non recourse. That's not just policy, it's the law.

## Take 3 — How a loan works (patient, explaining)  (~4 min)

Here's a pawn loan in one breath. The customer leaves an item with us and walks out with cash. Every pawn is a thirty day loan. When it comes due, they have three choices. Pick it up. Pay the finance charge and start another thirty days. Or let it go. There's no credit check, it never touches their credit, and it's confidential. That's the product, and it's a good one.

Two words customers mix up all the time. The principal is what we loaned them. The finance charge is the cost of borrowing it, which is the interest, the storage fee and the service fee added together. When a customer pays the finance charge, they buy another thirty days. It doesn't make the loan any smaller. Somebody who's paid the finance charge for a year still owes the same principal they started with. Say it plainly, then ask them to say it back to you.

Virginia allows four charges on a pawn, and each one has a cap. Interest is capped by the size of the loan: ten percent a month up to twenty five dollars, seven percent between that and a hundred, five percent at a hundred and up. Storage is no more than five percent of the loan a month. The service fee is the smaller of five percent or three dollars. The late fee is up to ten percent of the loan, and only if it's printed on the ticket. There's also a five dollar lost ticket fee, same condition. That's the whole list. No store adds its own fee. Bravo does the math. You never work it out by hand, and you never negotiate it.

Two rules keep this honest. First, you never split one loan into smaller loans to get a higher rate. A hundred and twenty dollars is one loan at the hundred dollar rate, not five little ones. Second, an extension is written on the original loan amount. Last month's interest and storage never get rolled into a new loan on the same item. And if you ever hear someone mention a flat twelve and a half percent in the grace period, that's gone. It's withdrawn. Bravo computes the real figure. If Bravo's number ever looks wrong, stop and call Preston. Don't make up a percentage.

Now the timeline. The loan is written on day zero and it's due thirty days later. Virginia gives a fifteen day grace period, so nothing can be sold before day forty five of the loan. That's the legal floor, not our pull date. Our hold runs longer. We hold the item through thirty days past the due date. On day thirty one it can be pulled into inventory, and pulling is finished by day sixty one. Starting in October, nothing is held past that without Joshua's approval. One exception: a loan where the customer never made a single payment is a first payment default, and it's expired as soon as it's past due. So you always check the account before you promise anything. And no item gets pulled without a statement of ownership on file. If one's missing, it's held and Preston hears about it that day.

This is the part customers are most afraid of, so get it right. A pawn loan is non recourse. The item is the whole security. If the customer doesn't come back, we keep the item and that's the end of it. We don't sue. We don't send it to collections. We don't report it to a credit bureau. We don't chase them if the item sells for less than the loan. So you never say, or even hint, that they owe us money, that they could be sued, or that their credit will take a hit. It isn't true, and saying it can break federal collection rules and Virginia's consumer protection law.

## Take 4 — Buying, gold & pricing (practical, matter-of-fact)  (~4 min)

Before you look up a single price, ask three questions. Have you done business with us before? Are you looking to loan it or sell it? And how much are you needing? Preston's words on this are simple: qualify it before you look anything up. It's pawnbroker one oh one. The answers change everything that comes after. A regular with a great payback record, a first-timer, someone who needs forty dollars and someone who needs four hundred all get handled differently.

Here's what the customer gets either way, in plain words. A loan gets them cash today and keeps their right to come back for the item. It's a thirty day loan. They pay to redeem it, or pay the finance charge and start another thirty days. There's no credit check, it never touches their credit, and it's private. And if they don't come back, the item settles it. They never owe us another dime. A buy gets them more cash today, and the item becomes ours. They sign, date and fingerprint the same contract, and we hold the item thirty days before we sell it. Whatever the charges are, Bravo figures them and they match the printed ticket. You never quote a cost that's different, and you never negotiate a redemption amount.

Now the judgment call. Pull up the customer's history. Are they someone who comes back for their stuff, or someone who lets it go? Preston has flagged this more than once. A fifteen dollar B B gun loaned to a customer with a sixty percent default rate should have been a buy. A ten dollar loan to someone who forfeits everything should have been a buy. In his words, most people don't come back for ten dollar loans. A small loan ties up our money for at least forty five days before we can sell anything, and a lot of the time it defaults anyway. When it's small and the history says they won't be back, offer the buy.

When you present, give both numbers side by side, and make them different. On general merchandise, the buy is the bigger number. Preston's rule is that the buy should be more money than the loan, because that forces a real decision. Gold works off the estimator, and you'll get that in Level five. Loan to what the customer needs, not everything they want. They may want more than they need, and loaning to the need keeps the loan something they can actually pay back. If the item doesn't cover what they need, don't stretch the number. Ask what else they brought.

Listen for one word: keep. On a call in Lexington, a guy wanted to sell his crossbow, then said, I wholeheartedly don't want to sell it. The employee caught that and offered a loan instead. That's the right product for that customer. Someone who's done with the item and just wants the cash is a buy. Whichever it is, explain it before they sign. For a loan, that's what they're borrowing, what it'll cost, and what they'll owe to get it back. For a buy, walk them through the purchase contract before you write it up.

Every offer starts with research, and there's one right way to do it. You comp sold listings only. Ones that got at least one bid, in used condition. An asking price isn't a comp, it's a wish. And the top of the range usually means brand new with every accessory, which the item on your counter isn't. Then pull the Bravo Price Guide report, because it shows what other pawn shops are paying. Two more rules. Never assume something's worthless until you've looked. And never read a chatbot's guess to a customer as our appraisal. One store spent fourteen minutes doing that and still passed.

## Take 5 — Firearms & compliance (serious, steady — this is where it matters)  (~4 min)

Every one of our five stores holds its own federal firearms license. Not just Roanoke. All five. The license names the building, so it doesn't travel with a person or move with the store. Holding it means we answer to federal firearms law and the A T F on top of Virginia pawn law. That's why this level is a hard gate. You don't touch a firearm transaction of any kind until you've passed every lesson here, your manager has signed off your floor checks, and your gun safety certificate is verified.

Before the paperwork, the person. Nobody who is prohibited under federal law can handle or transfer firearms in our stores, and every counter role handles firearms. Virginia also requires the State Police to background check a dealer's employee before that employee transfers a gun. If something changes in your life that could make you a prohibited person, you tell Preston right away. And if you're under twenty one, check with the Market Manager before you handle anything that might be an assault firearm. Virginia's rules on those are in flux right now, so the current rule comes from Preston, not the news.

The Acquisition and Disposition record, the A and D book, is the list of every firearm that comes into the store and every one that leaves. A gun coming in is an acquisition. That's a pawn, a buy, a trade in, a transfer shipped to us, or a return. It has to be in the book by close of the next business day, and pawns, our most common one, get logged the day they come in. A gun going out is a disposition. A sale, a transfer, a redemption, or a return to a customer. Those go in within seven days. Both deadlines are federal law. And a gun that doesn't work gets logged exactly like one that does.

Here's the rule people get wrong. Forty-four seventy-threes and the A and D record are kept until the company stops doing business. Then they get handed to the A T F. You'll hear people say twenty years. Twenty years is not a shred date. It's only the age when old paper forms can be moved to separate storage. You never destroy a forty-four seventy-three or an A and D record, and nothing gets destroyed at all without Joshua's approval. You'll also work with the store's binders: the daily gun log, the forty-four seventy-three log, the transfer log, and the multiple gun sales log.

If an A T F inspector or a police officer comes in, check their credentials and write down their name, their agency, and their badge or I D number. Then let them in. Right away. They're entitled to see our records and inventory during business hours, and you never make them wait for a manager. You text or call Preston and Joshua while you're helping, not before. If they ask something you don't know, say you don't know and offer to connect them with Preston or Joshua. Never guess.

If a gun is missing, from a count, a pawn walk, or the monthly gun audit, you tell Preston and Joshua the same hour. Not at the end of the shift. Federal law gives the company forty eight hours from when it's discovered to report it to the A T F and the police, and Joshua makes those reports. The regular seventy two hour incident report doesn't cover it, so don't wait on that paperwork. And the rule for this whole level: if anything about a firearm transaction feels off, stop and call Preston. A delay costs us almost nothing. Getting it wrong can cost us the license.

## Take 6 — Service & leadership (warm, encouraging)  (~4 min)

Things go wrong in this business. Somebody gets asked a question at the counter the wrong way. Somebody gets told the wrong thing. Something breaks a week after the warranty ends. The manual says it straight: indifference, more than rudeness, is the leading reason customers stop coming back. What they remember is what you did next. There are four moves. Let them vent. Apologize. Protect them. And own it.

When someone's upset, let them talk. Don't interrupt and don't defend. On one call, a man had been asked at the counter whether an award his mother gave him was really his. That question is part of our job, but the way it was asked hurt him. The employee on the phone let him get it all out, then apologized. You can apologize for how something made someone feel without saying the rule was wrong. If you don't know the answer, don't guess. Check with a coworker or your manager and tell the customer you're doing it. And never say Preston's not here, or that wasn't me. To the customer, you are the store.

Then protect them. That same caller admitted he's not a good haggler. Instead of taking advantage of it, the employee told him what we actually pay. The customer ended the call thanking us just for letting him come back. Protecting someone means you never take advantage of a person who's told you they're vulnerable. It means telling a caller who needs fifty dollars that her item is worth ten to fifteen, before she drives across town on false hope. And sometimes it's just going out of your way: calling a woman about the laptop she left on the counter before she even missed it, or holding a little boy's stuffed elephant, and the blue fish his dad forgot to mention.

When you get something wrong, own it out loud. Uriah told a customer a fee couldn't be waived. Then he checked with Preston and found out he was wrong. Nobody asked him to, but he called the customer back and said so. It cost the store the fee, and it earned us that customer. That's the standard. Find the right answer, call them back, and fix it.

Sometimes the answer is no, and that's fine. Say it clearly and early, with the real reason. Then give them something useful: who might buy it, or where it might sell. One Lexington employee spent a few minutes with a drummer trying to sell his own brand of drumsticks. He said no right away, explained why, then pointed him to a music store, lesson programs, Marketplace and eBay. The man said, thank you so much, that was very encouraging. And leave the door open. Tell them what we pay the most for, so next time they bring us that.

There are four things every customer who walks in hears from you. Our thirty day warranty. The clearance section. Free layaway. And the mobile app. Not just the people buying. The loan customer, the person making a payment, the person just looking around. That's in the manual, and Preston reminds us about it daily, because it's what sets us apart from every other shop in town.

Our layaway is free, and we mean free. No interest, no service fee, no holding fee. Never quote a customer a fee of any kind on a layaway. They put money down today, pay it off over time, and take it home when it's paid in full. The item gets tagged and set aside in Bravo so nobody can sell it out from under them. Every layaway has a signed agreement, and two receipts that both get signed. The original goes to the customer, the copy goes in the daily folder.

## Take 7 — Just talk (about 5 minutes, no script)

Talk naturally, like you're telling a new hire about Valley Pawn: how you got into the business, what makes a great day at the store, a customer story you like to tell, and what you expect from your team. This take matters — it captures how you actually sound.
