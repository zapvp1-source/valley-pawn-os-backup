What Does "Karat" Actually Mean?

Gold karat is a measure of purity — it tells you how much of the metal in a piece of jewelry is actually gold versus other metals mixed in. Pure gold is 24 karats, meaning it's 99.9% gold. But pure gold is very soft, so jewelers mix it with other metals like copper, silver, zinc, or nickel to make it stronger and more durable for everyday wear. The karat number tells you the ratio of gold to those other metals.

Think of it like a pie divided into 24 slices. If a ring is 14K, that means 14 of those 24 slices are pure gold and the remaining 10 are other metals. The higher the karat number, the more gold is in the piece — and generally, the more it's worth by weight.

Breaking Down the Common Gold Karats

24K Gold (99.9% pure) — This is as pure as it gets. You'll find 24K gold in some coins, bullion bars, and certain high-end Asian jewelry. It has a rich, deep yellow color but scratches and dents easily because it's so soft. It's rarely used for everyday rings or bracelets in the U.S.

18K Gold (75% pure) — A popular choice for fine jewelry, especially engagement rings and high-end pieces. It has a warm, rich color and holds up better than 24K for daily wear, though it can still scratch over time. You'll often see European and designer jewelry at this karat level.

14K Gold (58.3% pure) — This is the most common karat used in American jewelry. It strikes a great balance between durability and gold content, making it ideal for rings, necklaces, and bracelets that people wear every day. Most class rings, wedding bands, and chain necklaces you'll find are 14K.

10K Gold (41.7% pure) — The minimum karat that can legally be sold as "gold" in the United States. It's the most durable of the bunch because it has the highest percentage of alloy metals, making it a budget-friendly option that still holds real gold value. Many affordable jewelry lines use 10K gold.

How to Find the Karat Stamp on Your Jewelry

Most gold jewelry sold in the U.S. is required to carry a karat stamp, though very old or handmade pieces sometimes don't have one. Here's where to look: on rings, check the inside of the band. For necklaces and bracelets, look near the clasp or on a small tag attached to the chain. Earrings usually have the stamp on the post or back.

You might see the karat written a few different ways — "14K," "14KT," or "585" (which is the European hallmark for 14K, representing .585 gold purity). Similarly, "750" means 18K, "417" means 10K, and "999" means 24K.
