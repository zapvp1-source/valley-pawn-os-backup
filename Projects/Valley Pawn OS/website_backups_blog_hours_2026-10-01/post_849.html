What Actually Makes Silver Valuable

The value of most silver comes down to two things: how much it weighs and how pure it is. Silver purity is measured a little differently than gold. Instead of karats, you’ll usually see numbers like 925, which means the piece is 92.5% pure silver — the standard for “sterling silver.” Fine silver is stamped 999, and older coins often contain 90% silver. Costume jewelry marked “silver-tone” or “silver-plated,” on the other hand, has only a thin coating over a base metal and carries little melt value.

Once we know the purity, the rest is math. We weigh the piece, factor in the purity, and compare it to the current market price of silver that day. That’s why a heavier sterling gravy boat can be worth more than a delicate silver bracelet, even if the bracelet looks fancier.

How to Spot Real Silver at Home

Before you bring anything in, it helps to do a little detective work. Grab a magnifying glass and look for tiny stamps, called hallmarks. Common ones include 925, Sterling, 900, or 999. Coins are easy to research by year and country — many U.S. coins minted in 1964 or earlier contain 90% silver. And here’s a quick, harmless test: real silver is not magnetic. If a strong magnet sticks firmly to the piece, it’s likely not solid silver.

Common Silver Items People Sell

All kinds of everyday items turn out to hold silver value. The ones we see most often include:

  • Flatware and serving pieces — sterling forks, spoons, trays, and tea sets, often inherited and rarely used.
  • Coins — pre-1965 U.S. coins, silver dollars, and collectible rounds.
  • Jewelry — sterling rings, chains, bracelets, and brooches, including pieces that are damaged or missing a match.
  • Bars and rounds — investment-grade silver bought years ago and tucked away.
