What Actually Counts as Silver

Not everything that looks silvery is silver, and that’s the first thing worth understanding before you sell. Sterling silver is the standard for most jewelry, flatware, and serving pieces — it’s 92.5% pure silver alloyed with copper for durability, and it’s usually stamped with the marking “925,” “STERLING,” or “STER” somewhere on the piece. If you can’t find a mark, that doesn’t automatically mean it isn’t silver; older or handmade pieces sometimes lack stamps, which is why we test purity at the counter.

Coins live in a slightly different world. United States dimes, quarters, half-dollars, and dollars minted in 1964 or earlier were struck in 90% silver — they’re often called “junk silver” in the coin world, though there’s nothing junky about their melt value. Kennedy half-dollars from 1965 to 1970 are 40% silver. Modern bullion coins like the American Silver Eagle are 99.9% pure. Silver-plated items, on the other hand, are just a thin coating of silver over a base metal and generally have minimal scrap value — though certain antique plated pieces carry collector value well beyond the metal itself.

How Silver Pricing Works

Silver pricing starts with the daily spot price — the market value of one troy ounce of pure silver, which fluctuates throughout each trading day. From there, the offer on your piece is calculated based on its weight, its purity, and the current spot price, minus a small margin that covers refining, handling, and overhead. For sterling, that means roughly 92.5% of your item’s total weight contributes to its melt value. Glancing at the day’s spot price before you walk in (a quick web search will do it) gives you a useful benchmark for understanding any offer.

  • A government-issued photo ID

  • Any original receipts, appraisals, or certificates of authenticity, if you have them

One important note: we’ll happily buy tarnished silver, so please don’t try to polish it aggressively before bringing it in. Harsh polishing can actually damage value, especially on antique or hallmarked pieces where the patina and the marks themselves are part of the worth. A light dusting is plenty.
