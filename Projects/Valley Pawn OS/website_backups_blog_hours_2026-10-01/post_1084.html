Karat: How Pure Is the Gold?

Pure gold is 24 karat, but jewelry is almost never sold that soft — it would bend too easily. Most gold jewelry in the U.S. is 10k, 14k, or 18k, which tells you what percentage of the piece is actual gold mixed with other metals for durability. 14k gold, for example, is 14/24ths — about 58% — pure gold. The higher the karat, the more actual gold is in the piece, and the more it’s worth per gram.

Most jewelry is stamped somewhere discreet — inside a ring band, on a clasp, or on the back of a pendant — with a karat marking like “14K” or “585.” If we can’t find a stamp, we use an acid test or an electronic tester to verify purity on the spot, right in front of you.

Weight: Every Gram Counts

Once we know the purity, we weigh the piece on a certified scale, typically in grams or pennyweights. This is where a lot of customers are surprised — a small, dense ring can weigh more, and be worth more, than a bigger but hollow chain. Weight and karat together tell us exactly how much pure gold content you’re holding, which is the real basis for any fair offer.

One quick note: stones, clasps, and any non-gold components (like a watch back or a plated finish) don’t count toward the gold weight. If a piece has a diamond or other gemstone, we’ll appraise that separately — you’re not leaving that value on the table when you sell.

Market Price: Why the Number Changes Daily

Gold trades on a global market, and the “spot price” — what a troy ounce of pure gold is worth right now — moves throughout the day based on the economy, the dollar, and demand. Our appraisals use real-time pricing data, not a number we made up last month. That’s part of what it means to be a modern, tech-forward pawn shop: your offer reflects what the market actually says today, not what it said last year.

This is also why it can pay to check in more than once. If you got an offer somewhere a few months ago and passed, gold prices may have moved since then — sometimes in your favor. It never hurts to bring a piece back in and ask.

Getting the Best Offer

A few simple things help: bring pieces in clean (a quick rinse helps us see markings clearly), bring any documentation or appraisal paperwork you have, and don’t be afraid to ask questions about how we arrived at your number. A fair pawn shop will explain the math — karat times weight times market price — every time, no exceptions.
