Take a close look at the clasp of a gold necklace or the inside of a ring and you’ll usually find a tiny stamp — 10K, 14K, 18K, sometimes 24K. Those little marks are the single biggest factor in what your jewelry is worth. Understanding gold karat doesn’t require a jeweler’s degree, and once you know how to read those stamps, you’ll walk into any appraisal — including ours — with real confidence.

What Karat Actually Means

Karat is simply a measure of purity. Pure gold is 24 karat — 24 parts out of 24. But pure gold is soft, almost like stiff clay, so jewelers mix it with stronger metals like copper, silver, and zinc to make pieces that survive daily wear. A 14K ring is 14 parts gold and 10 parts other metals, which works out to about 58.3% pure gold. An 18K piece is 75% gold. A 10K piece — the legal minimum to be called gold jewelry in the United States — is just under 42%.

That’s why two rings that look nearly identical can have very different values. The gold karat stamp tells you how much actual gold is in the metal, and actual gold content is what drives the price.

The Stamps You’ll See Most Often

Most American jewelry carries one of a handful of marks. 10K (sometimes stamped 417) is durable and affordable — common in class rings and everyday chains. 14K (585) is the sweet spot for most American jewelry: a good balance of purity and strength. 18K (750) is richer in color and more valuable, often found in higher-end pieces. 24K (999) is pure gold, more common in coins and bars than in jewelry.

You may also spot GP (gold plated), GF (gold filled), or HGE (heavy gold electroplate). These pieces contain only a thin layer of gold over base metal, so they carry little melt value — though some still have worth as fashion jewelry. And occasionally a stamp is missing entirely or worn away. That doesn’t mean the piece isn’t gold; it just means it needs to be tested.

How Karat Translates to Dollars

When you sell gold or use it as collateral for a pawn loan, the math starts with three things: the gold karat, the weight, and the current market price of gold. A heavier piece at a higher karat is worth more — it’s that direct. An 18K bracelet weighing 20 grams contains nearly twice the pure gold of a 10K bracelet of the same weight, so it commands a meaningfully higher offer.

One thing worth knowing: gemstones, craftsmanship, and brand names can add value beyond the metal, especially for designer pieces or fine diamonds. A good appraiser looks at the whole piece, not just the scale.
