    How the three numbers are defined

    This splits the "Yield" the bonus program already uses — it does not redefine it.

    Loan yield
    interest + fees + misc charges collected this month loan balance at the end of last month
    Inventory yield
    gross profit on everything sold this month (sales − cost) inventory balance at the end of last month
    Blended — unchanged, this is the bonus-program "Yield"
    net revenue = loan revenue + retail gross profit loan balance + inventory balance, end of last month

    The split is exact, not approximate

    Layaway: the honest answer

    Layaway does not have a yield, and giving it one would count the same money twice. Here is why — proven from the data, not argued.

    Layaway is not a third asset class. It is a payment plan on the inventory you already own. When a customer puts an item on layaway, that item stays in your inventory until they pick it up. The "layaway balance" is not something the business owns — it is what the customer still owes on a sale that has not closed yet.

    So the money at risk is the cost of the goods sitting in the back, which is already inside the inventory balance. And the return is the gross profit when it gets picked up, which is already inside retail gross profit. Both sides are counted already.

    Bravo's End-of-Month sales summary — every store, every month:

        Taxable Sales + Taxable Layaway + Taxable Fees
     +  Nontaxable Sales + Nontaxable Layaway + Nontaxable Fees
     =  Total Sales

    Tested across 260 store-months → holds to the penny, zero mismatches.
    Layaway is a median 9% of total sales.

    And gross profit = Total Sales − cost of goods.
    → Layaway profit is already inside the inventory yield.

    Measure collection velocity instead. The real question on layaway is not what it earns, but how fast it turns into cash and how much is going stale. That is a genuine number worth watching — it just belongs underneath inventory, not beside it.

    One thing worth fixing in the weekly layaway report

    The weekly #layaway-review post divides part of a month's collections by a full balance. So the same store looks about half as good when pulled on the 14th as on the 30th — the number tracks the calendar, not performance. Using a full month's collections over the prior month's closing balance, as the table above does, makes the weeks comparable.

    Gold: a quarter of the cost base, a fifth of the earnings

    Scrap is not a separate asset class either — but unlike layaway, it is big. Refining is where a quarter of all cost of goods goes, it is growing fast, and it behaves like a different business from everything else on this page.

    The metal you send to the refiner leaves the inventory balance the same way a retail sale does — Bravo books it as "Refined (Cost of Sales)". So the capital is already counted inside the inventory denominator.

    And the refinery money is already inside the sales figure too. That was worth checking rather than assuming, because if the payout were missing, every heavy-refining month would look artificially unprofitable. It doesn't. See the test below.

    The test: is the refinery money actually in the sales figure?

    If refining proceeds were missing, gross margin would collapse in the months with heavy refining — you would be booking the cost of the metal with no revenue against it.

    Across 100 store-months, the opposite is true. Months where refining was under 10% of cost of goods averaged 50.6% gross margin. Months where it was over 35% averaged 53.7%. The correlation runs mildly positive (+0.28). Scrap is at least as profitable as the retail floor — it is not a hole in the numbers.

    What the melt is actually worth — the 4 August 2026 settlement

    Elemetal (Norfolk) paid $66,160.08 net on 608.13 pennyweight across all ten open buckets — a blended lot covering stones and no-stones together, settled by wire against two lots and $37.70 of shipping, processing and wire fees. That is $108.79 per pennyweight.

    The cost basis of that metal was roughly $27,000–29,000 — so a single monthly refinery cheque produced about $37,000 of gross profit at roughly a 57% margin. For scale: the whole company averages $224,000 of net revenue a month.

    Extending that across 2026 volume — 4,738 pennyweight year to date, up 31% on last year — the melt channel is producing somewhere in the region of $250,000–$340,000 of gross profit. That is 14–19% of everything the company earns, and roughly a quarter to a third of all retail gross profit. It is a bigger business than it looks from the org chart.

    The question this raises: melt or sell?

    Every pennyweight that goes in the pot is a pennyweight that did not go in the case. On the sold-jewellery records where a weight is actually stated, retail realises around $146 per pennyweight against the melt's $109 — roughly 1.3×. That is directional only: just 32 sold items across 17,000 carried both a gold marker and a weight in the description, so it is a signal, not a finding.

    It matters because scrap intensity is climbing — from 22.3% to 27.6% of cost of goods in a year. Two very different stories fit that: gold ran up and melting got more profitable, or merchandise that should have been retailed went to the pot instead. The data cannot yet separate them. Making descriptions carry karat and weight consistently would settle it, and it is the single highest-value data fix available here.

    The weight check — and the one that actually matters

    On the August cycle the refiner's pre-melt weight came in at 624.90 pennyweight against the store records' 608.13 — about 2.7% higher. That direction is in the company's favour: they acknowledged receiving more metal than the books said went out. The alarming direction would be the reverse.

    It is also probably not a like-for-like comparison. The refiner's figure is gross weight received before melting; the store figure is what was recorded at intake. With stones, clasps and findings in half the lots, those two are measuring different things — and it is a single settlement, so it is a data point, not a pattern.

    Gross weight is not where a refiner shorts you anyway — the assay is. The number to watch each cycle is the one the closeout procedure already names: actual dollars received against the system's estimated funds receivable, where 92–102% is the normal band. Anything landing consistently at the bottom of that band, or below it, is the real signal.

    Concentration and price risk

    Roughly a quarter to a third of all cost of goods now exits through one counterparty, settled in a single monthly wire. Two things follow. First, payout is spot-linked and unhedged — a sharp gold correction compresses about a sixth of company earnings at once. The "gold lock" a competing refiner quoted earlier this year is a hedge against exactly that, and is worth pricing now that this channel is this large.

    Second, refiner due diligence is worth doing properly for a licensed, BSA-obligated pawn operation. Confirm which Elemetal entity is on the other side of these wires and its current compliance standing before the next cycle — the corporate group has a federal anti-money-laundering history worth understanding rather than assuming.

    Where the numbers come from

    Every figure comes from Bravo's End-of-Month export — the same file the weekly KPI and bonus-target tasks already pull. Nothing is pulled from Bravo for this report, and no existing report was touched.

    Two independent checks run every time. Net revenue matches Preston's June 2026 commission basis to the penny for all five stores. And August 2026 matches the figures the bonus engine worked out on a completely separate path. Two pipelines, same answer.

    The August problem, found and fixed

    Both Augusts originally read as garbage — about 11 times too high. The cause was not a bad pull. Bravo names each export by its end date only. The monthly analytics job pulls six different date windows per store — this month, year-to-date, and trailing twelve months, for both this year and last — and every one of them ends on the same day. Six pulls, one filename. The last one written quietly replaced the real August file.

    That makes it a race rather than a predictable bug: any month can lose, and 10 of 265 files had. Balances survive it, because they are a point-in-time snapshot. Revenue figures do not.

    Both Augusts were recovered in full from copies the analytics job already saves under date-stamped names — no re-pull, no Bravo contact. From now on every export is also copied into a date-stamped archive as soon as it lands, so no pull can overwrite another's data again, and every file is checked against its own date header before a single number is read from it. Nothing in the existing pipeline was changed.

    Six checks have to pass before anything publishes

    The monthly refresh publishes nothing unless all six pass: the Preston match, the bonus-engine match, the sales identity that underpins the layaway call, every file covering its true month, the loan and inventory split adding back to the blended number on every row, and all five stores present. If a check fails, the report is withheld rather than published with a footnote.
